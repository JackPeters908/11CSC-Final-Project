Digital wellbeing among New Zealand teenagers is currently impacted by several interconnected challenges, most notably chronic sleep disruption, cyberbullying, anxiety, and harmful social comparison. Late-night "vampire scrolling" suppresses melatonin and keeps the mind active, leading to widespread fatigue that impairs daily academic performance and emotional regulation. At the same time, constant digital connectivity exposes rangatahi to cyberbullying—ranging from targeted harassment to group exclusion—without the safety of an offline boundary, an issue continuously monitored by local organizations like Netsafe. Furthermore, algorithmic feeds and highly curated highlight reels frequently drive upward social comparison and FOMO, which can exacerbate anxiety and depression by trapping vulnerable youth in negative feedback loops and unrealistic expectations.

A widespread myth is that screen time is inherently toxic, treating all digital engagement—whether interactive study sessions, creative projects, or passive doomscrolling—as equally damaging to mental health. Popular narratives often blame devices directly for rising rates of depression and anxiety, assuming that screen use inevitably replaces real-world relationships and destroys interpersonal skills. This oversimplification overlooks how digital platforms can actually serve as vital tools for maintaining friendships and finding supportive communities.

In reality, scientific research shows that total screen time has a minimal, indirect impact on mental health compared to foundational factors like sleep, exercise, and family dynamics. What matters most is the nature of the screen use: active, connection-focused activities tend to foster well-being, whereas passive consumption and constant algorithmic feeds are more strongly linked to lower mood. Ultimately, screens pose the greatest risk when they directly displace physical health needs like adequate rest and outdoor activity.

The popular narrative that social media is entirely harmful and that people are merely "scrolling their lives away" is an oversimplification, as research demonstrates that digital platforms function as a double-edged sword. While mindless scrolling and engagement with persuasive algorithms can lead to negative impacts such as sleep disruption and harmful social comparison, social media also offers significant psychological benefits. For many young people, these platforms provide crucial spaces for identity exploration, creative self-expression, and maintaining real-world connections, as well as offering access to peer support and mental health awareness. Ultimately, social media is neither purely beneficial nor completely destructive; its impact on a person's well-being depends on how it is used, where active, connection-focused interaction supports mental health, whereas passive consumption tends to drive negative outcomes.

Honestly, calling our whole generation "addicted" to social media feels like a bit of an exaggeration, even if most of us spend way too much time on our phones. Real addiction is a serious clinical issue that completely turns someone's life upside down, whereas most of us are just reacting to apps that were literally engineered by experts to keep us hooked using infinite scrolling and crazy personalized feeds. Calling it an addiction makes it sound like we're totally helpless, which ignores the fact that social media actually gives us a lot of good stuff—like staying connected with friends, finding cool niche communities, or just expressing ourselves. At the end of the day, we aren't facing some hopeless epidemic; we just need to get better at setting boundaries and realizing when an algorithm is trying to control our time.
